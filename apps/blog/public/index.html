Why browning tastes like that

2 September 2026  ·  about 8 minutes

For years I thought browning was one thing. Hot pan, food goes brown, food tastes better. That is true enough to cook by, and it is wrong in a way that costs you dinners.

There are two reactions doing the browning, and they want different things from you.

Caramelisation is sugar on its own

Caramelisation is what sugar does when it gets hot enough by itself. Nothing else has to be present. Table sugar starts breaking apart somewhere around 160 C, and the fragments recombine into hundreds of new compounds that read as nutty, bitter, slightly fruity, slightly burnt in the way you want.

That is a caramel sauce. It is the dark edge of a roasted parsnip and the lacquer on a creme brulee. Sweet things going deeper into themselves.

The Maillard reaction needs a partner

The Maillard reaction needs two things in the same place at the same time: a reducing sugar and an amino acid, which is to say a protein. It is a reaction between them, first described by Louis Camille Maillard in 1912 while he was looking into something else entirely.

It starts cooler than caramelisation, from about 140 C at the surface, and it produces a different and much larger family of compounds. Roast beef crust. Toast. Chicken skin. Coffee. Soy sauce. The smell of bread coming out of an oven, which is not a sweet smell at all.

A steak has a little sugar and a lot of protein. So do onions, so does bread dough, so does a scallop. That is why all of them brown into something savoury and complicated rather than simply sweet.

The quick test in a kitchen: if the thing browning contains protein, most of what you are smelling is Maillard. If it is more or less pure sugar, it is caramelisation. Onions do both at once, which is why they are so good, and why they take so long.

Water is the thing standing in your way

Here is the part that changed how I cook.

Both reactions need the surface of the food to be hot. Water will not let it get hot. While there is liquid water sitting on your steak, that surface stays at about 100 C, because every bit of energy you add goes into turning water into steam rather than into raising the temperature. And 100 C is not enough. Not for Maillard, nowhere near for caramelisation. Nothing browns. You get grey, and you get that sad squeak.

So the fastest route to a brown crust is not a hotter pan. It is a drier surface.

  • Pat it dry. Paper towel, both sides, twice, right before it goes in.
  • Salt it and leave it uncovered in the fridge for an hour, or overnight if you thought ahead. The salt pulls moisture to the surface, then the dry fridge air takes it away.
  • Do not crowd the pan. Six mushrooms in a 24 cm pan will brown. Twenty mushrooms steam each other, because all that water has nowhere to go.
  • Let the pan get properly hot first, then leave the food alone for two minutes. Every time you move it you cool the contact patch.

Wet food in a hot pan is the most common reason a home cook's steak does not look like the picture.

Hotter is not always faster

The opposite mistake is the ripping hot pan. Both reactions speed up with temperature, but so does burning, and burning is not a later stage of browning. It is a different set of compounds, they taste bitter, and you cannot walk them back.

The useful range for a crust is roughly 150 C to 200 C at the surface. Above that the outside blackens before the inside has done anything at all. This is why a good steak cook lifts the pan off the heat for a moment, and why onions for a proper soup take forty minutes on medium instead of eight on high. Colour is not the same thing as heat. Colour is heat multiplied by time, minus whatever the water stole from you.

What this changes on a Tuesday

  • Dry the surface. Always. It costs ten seconds.
  • Give things room. Two batches beat one crowded pan every time.
  • Season early for the drying, then again at the end for the taste.
  • If it is browning and it smells savoury, it is Maillard, and it wants moderate heat and patience.
  • If it is nearly pure sugar and going dark, it is caramelisation, and it moves from perfect to ruined in about fifteen seconds. Stay with the pan.

None of this is new chemistry, and cooks were doing it well for centuries before Maillard gave it a name. But the name helps. Once you can tell the two reactions apart, you stop asking your pan for more heat and start asking your ingredient for a dry surface.
