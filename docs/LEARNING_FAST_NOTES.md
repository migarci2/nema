# Source notes: the principles of learning fast

Working notes the owner wrote from "The Principles of Learning Fast" (Party
at the Moontower, Kris Abdelmessih) and Justin Skycak's essays on Math
Academy. They are the source material for nema's philosophy and for the
learner model. Sources are listed at the end; claims should be attributed to
them, not invented.

1. Learning fast means maximising knowledge acquired, retained and usable per
   unit of time and effort, not consuming information fast. Remove every
   piece of work that does not build or stabilise a skill.
2. Most people's practical ceiling sits far below their real one. An
   exceptional tutor moves it by removing years of practising the wrong
   thing, building on weak prerequisites, being stuck, restudying what is
   known, forgetting, and choosing badly what to learn next. The part that
   can be automated is individualised instruction, not motivation or mentors.
3. Working memory is the bottleneck. Automatised lower skills free working
   memory for higher reasoning (the basketball player does not think about
   dribbling).
4. Automaticity matters more than it looks: practice to retrieval to
   automaticity lowers cognitive load and lets you handle harder problems.
5. Memory decays. "I understood it once" is not "I know it". Re-exposure
   (reading again) gives familiarity; retrieval (producing the answer with
   the material closed) strengthens memory.
6. Spaced repetition: retrieve when it has become effortful but is still
   recoverable; each success lets the next interval grow (1d, 3d, 7d, 14d,
   30d as a shape, individual in practice).
7. Flashcards treat knowledge as independent units; maths, programming and
   physics are hierarchical. Practising D also practises C, B and A.
8. Prerequisite graph (what you need before) versus encompassing graph (what
   you implicitly practise while doing something more advanced). Math Academy
   uses partial relations: Fractional Implicit Repetition (FIRe).
9. Theoretical maximum efficiency: if each new skill fully encompasses the
   previous ones, new learning is the spaced repetition of everything below.
   Real curricula sit between that and independent flashcards. A good
   curriculum reuses earlier knowledge on purpose.
10. Mastery learning: do not teach X until its prerequisites are mastered.
    There is no "class level", only a distribution of knowledge profiles.
11. Work at the edge of mastery: not too easy, not too hard, just outside the
    current repertoire.
12. Deliberate practice is mindful repetition: many attempt, feedback,
    adjustment cycles. Mindless drill fails; one hour on one problem fails.
13. Very hard problems are a poor way to acquire skills: thirty calibrated
    problems with feedback beat one olympiad problem. Build bridges, not
    bigger jumps.
14. Worked examples for novices, then fade the scaffolding.
15. Direct instruction beats discovery for beginners (expertise reversal):
    experts can explore because they hold thousands of schemas.
16. Projects and open problems are for creativity once a base exists; do not
    confuse the mechanism for acquiring skills with the one for exercising
    them.
17. Interleave practice (A, B, C, A, D, B) so you must choose the method,
    not just execute it. It feels worse and works better (desirable
    difficulty).
18. Avoid interference: do not introduce many confusable new things at once.
19. The illusion of understanding: rereading measures recognition; you need
    recall and application. Good techniques feel worse because they expose
    failures.
20. Learning styles are a myth; the representation follows the content.
21. Effort is not learning. Distinguish desirable difficulty from
    unproductive difficulty caused by missing prerequisites.
22. Structural integrity: advanced knowledge stresses earlier knowledge;
    people understand algebra better after calculus.
23. School is fixed time, variable mastery; talent development is variable
    time, fixed mastery.
24. Doing this by hand does not scale to thirty students; that is why the
    principles are old and still rare in classrooms.
25. AI tutoring is learning management, not text generation: decide what to
    learn now, what you are not ready for, what you forgot, what is
    automatic, where the hole is, how much to practise, when to help.
26. A serious tutor keeps an explicit student model over a knowledge graph
    with a forgetting model, and picks the next task (an expert system, not
    a chatbot).
27. Minimum effective dose of instruction and of practice, interleaved:
    micro explanation, problem, feedback, problem.
28. Frequent knowledge audits: small assessments to update the profile and
    localise errors; an error is a node needing intervention, not minus one.
29. Competence drives confidence drives motivation drives practice.
30. Technology removes wasted effort, not productive effort; aim for
    productive work over total work approaching one.
31. Much edtech is soft because engagement is not learning; accountability
    cuts both ways.
32. These principles are decades old and feel new because they feel worse
    during practice, show worse short term performance, and cost a teacher
    too much to individualise.
